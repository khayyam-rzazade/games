For researchers

Every question in Find out about yourself comes from a published research scale and is shown word for word, with its published answer scale. What is ours (the names, the one-line instructions, a few fillers, the country names) is marked. Every small change for the phone is in the Deviations table. Scores are worked out by the rules below, the same code on the phone and on the server. Answers are kept only for players who say yes at the end, without a name, an email or an address; the anonymised data will be released after the first paper, coarsened so no one can be picked out. Questions: logicers.world@gmail.com

Find out about yourself — item sheet (version 1.1, 22 October 2026: plain game and band names, the society ladder shown last, deviations D23–D24; version 1 on 21 October)

Every scored item and its response format is shown exactly as published; what is ours (names, instructions, fillers, country names) is marked. Every deviation from the published instrument is in the Deviations table at the end. Sources were opened and read on 21 October 2026; where only a copy or a secondary source could be opened, this is said.

Section name on the site: "Find out about yourself…". Nine games, about 62 taps, about six minutes. Player-facing names hide the construct; the construct is named once on each reveal ("What this looked at") and here.

Conventions on screen (ours, not part of any instrument): one item per screen; tappable dots; the two ends of every scale shown in the published words; the tapped value read back as "4 of 5", "7 of 10" or "60%"; a Skip button on every item; Back allowed within a game. Skip is coded as missing (ISSP "Can't choose", ESS "Don't know/Refusal", ANES no answer). A game scores when at least two-thirds of its scored items are answered. A random anonymous ID; nothing leaves the phone before consent.

Game 1 — "Your personality" · Big Five personality · BFI-10

Source. Rammstedt, B., & John, O. P. (2007). Measuring personality in one minute or less: A 10-item short version of the Big Five Inventory in English and German. Journal of Research in Personality, 41, 203–212. Appendix read in full (author copy); cross-checked with the GESIS ZIS documentation.

Published instruction (shown under "?"): "How well do the following statements describe your personality?" Stem (on every screen): I see myself as someone who…

# Item (verbatim) Trait Keyed
1 … is reserved Extraversion R
2 … is generally trusting Agreeableness +
3 … tends to be lazy Conscientiousness R
4 … is relaxed, handles stress well Neuroticism R
5 … has few artistic interests Openness R
6 … is outgoing, sociable Extraversion +
7 … tends to find fault with others Agreeableness R
8 … does a thorough job Conscientiousness +
9 … gets nervous easily Neuroticism +
10 … has an active imagination Openness +

Response format (verbatim): 1 Disagree strongly · 2 Disagree a little · 3 Neither agree nor disagree · 4 Agree a little · 5 Agree strongly. All five labels shown as words. Scoring (published): reverse 1, 3, 4, 5, 7 (6 − x); each trait = mean of its two items, 1–5. Order fixed as published. Shown to the player: five bars named Outgoing (E), Warm (A), Organised (C), Calm (reversed N), Curious (O); no bands. Guess before the game: "How outgoing are you?" (Quiet ⟷ Outgoing), stored as the self-insight gap for E. Not used: the paper's optional eleventh Agreeableness item ("… is considerate and kind to almost everyone"). The authors recommend it when Agreeableness is central; here it is a correlate, and ten items is already the one exception to the site's up-to-nine rule. Documented.

Game 2 — "Who should decide?" · Populist attitudes · Akkerman, Mudde & Zaslove (2014)

Source. Akkerman, A., Mudde, C., & Zaslove, A. (2014). How populist are the people? Measuring populist attitudes in voters. Comparative Political Studies, 47(9), 1324–1353, Table 1. Read from a full-text copy of the published article; cross-checked against the authors' Dutch summary (Res Publica, 2015) and the reproduction in Castanho Silva et al. (2020, APSR supplement).

Country tap first (ours): "Are you Italian?" (the header country) · Yes · No → "Where are you from?" (optional) list with flags; Skip allowed. The chosen country fills [Parliament] here and in game 7 and is the player's country in every comparison; the header country is stored beside it. If skipped, the header country fills the names and the player's country is recorded as "not said".

Stem: none. Instruction (ours): How much do you agree?

# Item (verbatim, POP1 localised)
POP1 The politicians in [Parliament] need to follow the will of the people.
POP2 The people, and not politicians, should make our most important policy decisions.
POP3 The political differences between the elite and the people are larger than the differences among the people.
POP4 I would rather be represented by a citizen than by a specialized politician.
POP5 Elected officials talk too much and take too little action.
POP7 What people call "compromise" in politics is really just selling out on one's principles.

[Parliament] = the everyday English name of the player's national parliament from the institution table ("the Dutch parliament" in the original; e.g. "Parliament" for Italy, "the Bundestag", "Congress"). The original's definite article is kept where the name takes one.

Response format (verbatim ends): 1 "I very much disagree" to 5 "I very much agree"; points 2–4 are unlabelled in the paper; on screen they carry our words disagree · in between · agree (D23) and are read back as "4 of 5". Scoring (published): mean of the six items (the authors' populism scale: POP1–POP5 and POP7; POP6 did not load, POP8 was dropped in the original analysis). Higher = more populist. Order: shuffled per play, positions recorded (ours; the original was a web panel grid). Bands (ours, on the 1–5 mean): With the experts (< 2.67) · In the middle (2.67–3.66) · With the people (≥ 3.67). Bands are display only; the raw mean is the record. Known caveats for the paper: all six items are positively worded (acquiescence); later work (Castanho Silva et al. 2020) finds the scale strong on people-centrism and anti-elitism and weak on the Manichaean element; POP4 is sensitive to translation (English only in wave 1); in Flanders POP6 (good versus evil) loaded only .32, and the authors replaced it with two anti-establishment items (Spruyt et al. 2016), which fits our use of POP1–5 and POP7.

Find out about yourself — scoring rules (codebook part 1)

Rules version 1, 21 October 2026 (band names made plain on 22 October; the edges and everything else unchanged, so the version stays 1). Written from the item sheet (claude/find-outs-items.md, version 1) plus the three rules Khayyam settled on 21 October (marked K). The other open questions from the data pass (S1, S2, S4) turned out to change no possible score; they are settled in G2 and G6. The code is claude/find-outs-scoring/scoring.js; every result carries rules_version: "1" so the dataset always says which rules made a score. If a rule changes, the version goes up and old answers can be re-scored from the raw record.

The raw answers are the record. Scores and bands are derived and can always be rebuilt from the raw answers.

General rules

G1. Answered or not. An item is answered when it has a valid value. A skipped item is stored as null. An item that was never shown (the European Parliament item outside the EU) is left out of the answers. Both count as not answered.

G2. When a game scores (the two-thirds rule). A game scores when at least two-thirds of its scored items are answered, rounded up: needed = ⌈2 × scored items ÷ 3⌉. Fillers and the attention check are not scored items and never count.

Game Scored items Needed
1 Big Five 10 7
2 Populist attitudes 6 4
3 Optimism 6 4
4 Child-rearing 4 3
5 Conspiracy mentality 5 4
6 Riddles 4 3
7 Trust (band items) 6 4
8 Chauvinism index 4 3
9 Quick ones — no rule; each item stands alone

A game that does not score gives no score and no band (both null); the player can redo it at once (plan rule 10).

G3. Partial answers (K, 21 Oct). When a game scores but some items were skipped, the score is the average of the answered items, put back on the published scale. With nothing skipped this gives exactly the published score. Two exceptions, G4 and G5.

G4. Big Five pairs (K, 21 Oct). Each Big Five trait needs both of its items. If one is skipped, that trait is left out (null) and the other traits still show. Reason: each pair has one reversed item, so one item alone leans toward yes-saying.

G5. Riddles (K, 21 Oct). A skipped riddle counts as not correct. The score is the number correct out of four. Skipping can never raise the score.

G6. Band edges are exact (S1 and S2, settled 21 Oct). Bands are decided by exact arithmetic on whole-number sums, never by comparing a rounded decimal: - 2.67 and 3.67 (games 2 and 8) mean exactly 8/3 and 11/3. Every possible answer pattern gets the same band as rounding the mean to two decimals would give (checked by listing every pattern), so this changes nothing for players. It only avoids a computer comparing 3.6667 with 3.67 and getting it wrong. - "4–6.4" and "≥ 6.5" (games 5 and 7) are read as lower bounds: below 4; 4 up to but not including 6.5; 6.5 and above. No possible score falls between 6.4 and 6.5, so this also changes nothing. - The score itself is stored at full precision. Rounding is for display only, and a band is never worked out again from a rounded number.

G7. Bad input stops the scoring. An unknown item key, a value outside the scale, a non-whole number or an option that is not on the list raises an error. That way a bug in the game screens shows up in testing instead of quietly entering the data.

Game by game

Answers are given as an object: item key → value (or null for skipped).

Game 1 — Big Five (BFI-10)

  • Keys "1"–"10" (the published item numbers). Values: whole numbers 1–5.
  • Reversed: 1, 3, 4, 5, 7 → 6 − x.
  • Traits (mean of the two items after reversing): E = 1, 6 · A = 2, 7 · C = 3, 8 · N = 4, 9 · O = 5, 10.
  • Calm = 6 − N (the bar shown as "Calm").
  • Scores when at least 7 of 10 are answered (G2); then each trait follows G4.
  • No single score, no bands: score and band are null.

Game 2 — Populist attitudes (Akkerman, Mudde & Zaslove 2014)

  • Keys "POP1", "POP2", "POP3", "POP4", "POP5", "POP7". Values 1–5. No reversed items.
  • Score = mean of answered items, 1–5.
  • Bands: With the experts (below 8/3) · In the middle (8/3 up to but not including 11/3) · With the people (11/3 and above).

Game 3 — Optimism (LOT-R)

  • Keys "1"–"9" (our positions). Values 0–4.
  • Scored: positions 1, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9. Reversed: 3, 7, 8 → 4 − x. Fillers: 2, 5 (stored, not scored). Attention check: 6.
  • Score = sum of the six scored items, 0–24. With skips (G3): 6 × mean of the answered scored items (prorated: true).
  • Bands (ours; the LOT-R itself has no cut-offs): The realist (below 12) · Realist with hope (12 up to but not including 18) · The optimist (18 and above). With no skips these are exactly the item sheet's whole-number bands 0–11, 12–17, 18–24; with skips no possible score falls between 11 and 12 or between 17 and 18.
  • Attention check: passed only when position 6 is exactly 3 (agree). Anything else, including a skip, is "failed attention check". Flag only; it never changes the score or the screen.

Game 4 — Child-rearing values (ANES)

  • Keys "1"–"6" (positions). Value: the chosen option, written exactly as on the sheet.
  • Pairs and the authoritarian choice: 1 Independence / Respect for elders · 2 Curiosity / Good manners · 4 Obedience / Self-reliance · 5 Being considerate / Well behaved. Fillers: 3 (A big imagination / A good memory), 6 (One best friend / Lots of friends), stored, not scored.
  • Share = authoritarian choices ÷ answered pairs, 0–1 (the published 0–1 rescale when all four are answered).
  • Score = 4 × share, 0–4 (the published count when all four are answered).
  • Bands (ours): the score rounded to the nearest whole number, then the item sheet's bands: Freedom first (0–1) · A bit of both (2) · Rules first (3–4). With all four answered this is exactly the item sheet's rule. With three answered, 1 of 3 gives Freedom first and 2 of 3 gives Rules first, the same way 1 of 4 gives Freedom first and 3 of 4 gives Rules first. (A score can never end in .5, so there are no ties.)

Game 5 — Conspiracy mentality (CMQ)

  • Keys "1"–"5". Values 0–10 (0% = 0 … 100% = 10). No reversed items.
  • Score = mean of answered items, 0–10.
  • Bands: Trusts what is said (below 4) · Asks questions first (4 up to but not including 6.5) · Looks for secret plans (6.5 and above).

Game 6 — Riddles (CRT-2)

  • Keys "1"–"4". Value: the chosen option, written exactly as on the sheet ("Second", "8", "Emily", "None" are the correct ones). Optional key "seen_before": true, false or null.
  • Each riddle is coded correct / lure / other / skipped.
  • Score = number correct, 0–4; skipped counts as not correct (G5). The game still needs 3 of 4 answered to score (G2).
  • Bands: Quick answers (0–1) · Half and half (2) · Careful thinker (3–4).

Game 7 — Trust in institutions (ESS)

  • Keys "B6", "B7", "B8", "B9", "B10", "B11", "B12", "grandmother". Values 0–10.
  • Band items: B6–B10 and B12. B11 (European Parliament, EU only) and the grandmother filler are stored and reported, never in the score.
  • Score = mean of the answered band items, 0–10. A skipped B6 is fine as long as 4 of the 6 are answered (rule G2).
  • Bands: Careful with trust (below 4) · Trusts some (4 up to but not including 6.5) · Trusts a lot (6.5 and above).
  • Every item is also returned on its own, because each one is compared with the pools on its own.

Game 8 — National chauvinism (ISSP 2013, Q3)

  • Keys "Q3a"–"Q3f". Values: the ISSP codes 1–5 (1 = Agree strongly … 5 = Disagree strongly).
  • Chauvinism index = mean of (6 − code) over the answered items among Q3a, Q3c, Q3d, Q3e, 1–5, higher = more chauvinist.
  • Q3b and Q3f are returned as their raw ISSP codes, not in the index. Q3b is marked R on the item sheet: agreeing with it points away from chauvinism. It is reported as the raw code; the direction is set in the analysis, not here.
  • Bands: World citizen (below 8/3) · Country and world (8/3 up to but not including 11/3) · Country first (11/3 and above).

Game 9 — Quick ones

  • Keys "1"–"9". Values: 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 8 → 0–10; 3 (society ladder) → 1–10; 5 (interest in politics) → ESS codes 1 very · 2 quite · 3 hardly · 4 not at all interested; 9 (direction) → 1 right direction · 2 wrong direction.
  • No score, no bands, no two-thirds rule: each item is returned on its own.

What the code returns

Every game returns:

Field Meaning
game 1–9
rules_version "1"
total number of scored items (game 9: 9)
answered how many of those were answered
needed the two-thirds count (game 9: null)
scored true / false (game 9: null)
score the main score on the published scale, or null (game 1, game 9, or not scored)
band the band name, or null (no bands, or not scored)

Plus, by game: - 1: traits {E, A, C, N, O} and calm; each a number or null. - 3: mean (0–4), prorated (true only when the game scored with a skipped scored item; false otherwise, including when it did not score), attention_passed. - 4: share (0–1), count (authoritarian choices), pairs_answered. - 6: codes {"1"…"4": "correct" / "lure" / "other" / "skipped"}, seen_before. - 7: items {B6 … B12, grandmother}: each 0–10 or null. - 8: single {Q3b, Q3f}: raw ISSP code or null. - 9: items {"1"…"9"}: value or null.

When a game does not score, the extra fields that are scores (traits, calm, mean, share, count) are null (traits is a single null, not five); the ones that describe answers (codes, items, single, attention_passed, seen_before, pairs_answered) are still filled in.
