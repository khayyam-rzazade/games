Para quienes investigan

Cada pregunta de Descubre quién eres viene de una escala de investigación publicada y se muestra palabra por palabra, con su escala de respuesta publicada. Lo que es nuestro (los nombres, las instrucciones de una línea, algunas frases de relleno, los nombres de los países) está señalado. Cada pequeño cambio hecho para el teléfono está en la tabla de desviaciones. Las puntuaciones se calculan con las mismas reglas en el teléfono y en el servidor. Las respuestas se guardan solo de quienes dicen que sí al final, sin nombre, correo electrónico ni dirección; los datos anónimos se publicarán después del primer artículo, con menos detalle, para que nadie pueda ser reconocido. Find out about yourself — item sheet, Spanish (version 4, 26 October 2026; item_version 30)

Version 1 (24 October) was written without four documents obtained since. Version 2 (26 October) added them. Version 3 recorded three decisions on them. Version 4 restates the matching rule in the form that fits every case in this sheet — the version 3 wording ("the instrument's own translation stays; another survey's Spanish yields to the English item") contradicted the sheet's own game 2, where another survey's Spanish is kept. No item changed.

37 of the 57 scored items are in a published Spanish version word for word, and one more (dial 6) keeps a published question with our end labels. Version 2 said 40; three items moved back to our translation under the matching rule below, which is the right trade. (Version 1 said "25 of 48": both numbers were wrong — the section has 57 scored items, and v1 had 29 published.)

The Spanish companion to the English item sheet (English v1.1): the same nine games, the same scoring, the same bands (their Spanish names come from the copy doc, left blank here), the same conventions on screen (one item per screen, Skip, Back, the readout "4 de 5"). Only the words change. Everything in the English sheet's rules holds unless this file says otherwise.

How each text was chosen (in this order): 1. an official or authors' Spanish version; 2. a validated Spanish version printed in a peer-reviewed article; 3. our translation, back-translated into English beside it, marked in the Deviations table as "our translation, back-translated, not validated". The documents added on 26 October were read as files, so their text is exact: PDFs through pdftotext (layout mode), the two Word files by unpacking them and reading document.xml. Nothing is quoted from memory, and nothing is quoted from a search-result snippet: if it was not opened, it is not a source here.

A fourth category appears in this version and is marked wherever it is used: published and fielded in Spanish, but not the instrument's own translation — the ESS trust question and one ESS dial, asked in their own Spanish by an academic survey in Spain. That is better Spanish than ours and citable, but it is not what the ESS fields, so it does not close the benchmarking gap.

Matching the English version comes before using a published Spanish item. All nine games will be analysed across the three languages together, so the questions have to be the same question. Where a published Spanish wording would ask something the English sheet does not ask, we translate the English item instead and mark it ours. Applied in version 3, this changed three things:

  • dial 5 (interest in politics): the published ANES Spanish is the CSES item, whose four options are not the ESS's. Dropped; ESS B1 translated instead.
  • game 7, B7: the published Spanish says "el poder judicial" (the judiciary); the ESS asks about the legal system. Dropped; our "El sistema judicial" instead.
  • dial 6 (satisfaction with democracy): the published question matches ESS B30 and is kept; its 0–10 end labels ("Nada / Muy satisfecho") are replaced by ours, matching the ESS's "Extremely dissatisfied / Extremely satisfied".

What the rule bites on is a different question, not translation variation inside the right question. A published Spanish rendering of the item the English sheet uses stays, even where its words run looser than the English — game 8's Q3e ("tenga una posición incorrecta" for "is in the wrong"), POP1's "Los parlamentarios" for "members of parliament", POP5's "hablan mucho y hacen poco". The ISSP, the ANES, the BFI and the LOT-R all pool their own national translations in their own comparative work, these departures are inside what the instruments tolerate, and a published item is what a reviewer can check; replacing them with our Spanish would cost more in publishability than it buys in parallelism. What yields is a published item that is a different item (dial 5: the CSES's four options where the English sheet asks the ESS's) or that names a different object (B7: the judiciary where the ESS asks about the legal system). Stated that way the rule also leaves the Italian and Turkish sheets alone: ITANES's "la volontà dei cittadini" and the SOEP's Turkish "riziko" are translation variation inside the right item, so they stand and are not re-opened.

Deviation numbering. The Italian sheet continued the English table at D25 and the Turkish sheet did the same. This sheet also continues at D25, so the numbers repeat across languages: every row below is tagged ES. The codebook needs the language tag, not the number alone.

Which Spanish, and why it is no longer one answer. Game 8 exists in three official Spanish versions of the same module year, and they are not equally good — Spain's has three textual slips and one change of meaning (set out in game 8). So "use Spain's Spanish" is the wrong rule for that game, and the sheet uses the US-Spanish version, the ISSP's deliberately neutral Spanish for a mixed Hispanic readership, with Spain's and Mexico's printed item by item beside it. Elsewhere there is no choice to make: the BFI is pan-Spanish by construction, the LOT-R is Spain's, the populism battery and the trust question are Spain fieldings, the child-rearing pairs are US Spanish.

Game 5 — "Secret plans" · Conspiracy mentality · CMQ

Ours (D31 ES) — and the Spanish conspiracy items now in hand cannot be used. Both Olivas Osuna questionnaires contain Spanish conspiracy items, and one is tantalisingly close to CMQ item 5: "Hay organizaciones secretas con gran influencia sobre las decisiones políticas" (2023, Q9_62). It is not the CMQ. The CMQ is defined by its stem ("I think that…") and its eleven-point 0–100 % likelihood scale; these items are standalone statements on a five-point agree–disagree scale, the set is three items rather than five, and neither record attributes them to Bruder et al. The other two are a COVID-era item and an unrelated one. Taking a sentence that resembles one CMQ item out of a different instrument and calling the item published would be wrong, so game 5 stays our translation in full. Also searched and not reachable: a Spanish version on the authors' site (no such page); Imhoff et al. (2022, Nature Human Behaviour) 26-country questionnaires (restricted files). The Cameron & Williams honours report uses the CMQ in English (New Zealand sample) and prints no Spanish; it is useful reading for the research itself, not a source here.

No Spanish CRT-2 exists. Ours, back-translated (D32 ES). The structure, the lure, the answers and the four-option rule stay as in English.

# Item (ours) Correct Lure Two other wrong options Back-translation
1 Si en una carrera adelantas a la persona que va en segundo lugar, ¿en qué puesto quedas? Segundo Primero Tercero · Último If in a race you overtake the person in second place, what position are you in?
2 Un granjero tenía 15 ovejas y murieron todas menos 8. ¿Cuántas quedan? 8 7 15 · 0 A farmer had 15 sheep and all but 8 died. How many are left?
3 El padre de Lucía tiene tres hijas. Las dos primeras se llaman Abril y Mayo. ¿Cómo se llama la tercera hija? Lucía Junio Julio · Marzo Lucía's father has three daughters. The first two are called Abril and Mayo. What is the third daughter called?
4 ¿Cuántos metros cúbicos de tierra hay en un agujero de 3 m de profundidad, 3 m de ancho y 3 m de largo? Ninguno 27 9 · 3 How many cubic metres of earth are there in a hole 3 m deep, 3 m wide and 3 m long?

The names in riddle 3. The trap works because the first two names are months, so the mind runs on to the next one. In Spanish the months are Abril, Mayo, Junio. "Abril" is a real and fairly common girl's name; "Mayo" is not a name, but it is the second month of the series and the reader sees the series at once, which is what makes "Junio" pull — the same compromise the English riddle makes with April and May. The correct answer had to be a very common girl's name that is not a month: Lucía. "Julia" was avoided because it shares its root with "Julio" (July), one of the wrong options. The two other wrong options are the next and the previous month, as in English. Another option considered: "Las dos primeras se llaman Abril y Marcela" (a real name that echoes Marzo) — closer to real names, weaker trap. We recommend the months. Words that differ by country (flagged, D37 ES). Riddle 1 "adelantas a" is Spain's word for overtaking; Mexico would say "rebasas a", and a neutral option is "pasas a la persona que va en segundo lugar". Riddle 4 "agujero" is understood everywhere; much of Latin America says "hoyo". Riddle 2 "granjero" works everywhere. Riddle 4: metres, as in English (D15). The riddles use "tú", as riddles do in Spanish; "usted" would make them sound like a form. After the four (ours): ¿Habías visto antes alguno de estos acertijos? Sí · No. Explanations on the reveal: they are copy, not items; the Spanish copy doc has them.

  • Spain's Q3b changes the construct. The source item is "some things about [Country] make me feel ashamed of [Country]"; Spain's asks about being ashamed of being Spanish. That moves a critical-patriotism item onto identity, and it is the single item in game 8 where a national version says something the English does not.
  • Spain's Q3c carries a stray "]", drops "más" (more like), and writes "la gente … fuesen" (plural verb with a singular subject). Spain's Q3d drops "un mejor país" to "es mejor". These are typesetting and editing slips, not translation choices, but they are in the fielded document.
  • Mexico's Q3e says "soportar" — to put up with, to tolerate — where the item means "support". That is a translation error; Spain and the US both have "apoyar".
  • The US version is the one written to be read by Hispanics of every origin, which is what a worldwide Spanish site needs, and it is closest to the English on Q3a, Q3b and Q3d. Its weakest line is Q3e, where "tenga una posición incorrecta" is a paraphrase and Spain's "estuviese equivocado" is nearer.

Why the matching rule does not force a fix to Q3e. It is translation variation inside the right item, not a different item. These are the ISSP's own national translations, and the ISSP pools exactly these versions in its own cross-national analyses — a national version's paraphrase is inside what the instrument tolerates, and the published item is what a reviewer can check. Mixing one item from Spain into a US-Spanish battery would give one scale with items from two fieldings, which is worse for invariance than the paraphrase is. The battery is switched whole or not at all.

Coding direction, for the codebook. Mexico and the US print the scale 1 = Totalmente/Muy de acuerdo … 5 = Muy en desacuerdo, the ISSP standard. Spain's questionnaire prints the same words coded the other way round, 5 = Muy de acuerdo … 1 = Muy en desacuerdo, with NS = 8 and NC = 9; the archived ISSP data is harmonised to the standard. We use the ISSP standard (1 = agree strongly) and reverse for the chauvinism index so higher = more chauvinist, exactly as the English sheet says. A national questionnaire's printed numbers are its own field coding and never ours. Not used: Q3g and Q3h, which all three versions carry (Spain's g "A menudo estoy menos orgulloso de ser español de lo que me gustaría estar", h "El mundo sería un lugar mejor si los españoles reconocieron los defectos de España"), plus the Q4 pride list, Q12, Q1 and Q2 — as in English. Scoring, bands, order: as in English.
